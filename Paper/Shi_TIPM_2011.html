A Numerical Investigation of Transpiration Cooling with Liquid Coolant Phase Change
Tier 2 — Standard
J. X. Shi, J. H. Wang
Transport in Porous Media (Springer), Vol. 87, No. 3, 2011, pp. 703-716 · DOI: 10.1007/s11242-010-9710-9
Paper Summary
This paper develops a one-dimensional numerical model for transpiration cooling with liquid-coolant phase change inside a porous matrix by coupling the two-phase mixture model (TPMM) with a local thermal non-equilibrium (LTNE) energy formulation, replacing the conventional local-thermal-equilibrium (LTE) assumption. The matrix is divided into sub-cooled liquid, two-phase, and superheated vapor regions, with enthalpy and saturation used in place of fluid temperature so that one set of governing equations spans all three regions. Parametric simulations with pure water as coolant examine how coolant mass flow rate, heat flux, porosity, sphere diameter, solid thermal conductivity, and hot-surface pressure affect temperature and saturation distributions. A key finding is an inverse phenomenon: in the two-phase region the coolant (fluid) temperature can exceed the solid temperature, contrary to single-phase transpiration-cooling studies, and this inversion can only be captured by the LTNE-TPMM. The model also shows that higher hot-surface pressure worsens cooling because the latent heat of vaporization decreases.

Introduction (click for detail)
The introduction frames two-phase flow and heat transfer in porous media as a classical topic and reviews how the separate phase model (SPM) gave way to the more convenient two-phase mixture model (TPMM). It notes that transpiration cooling has drawn attention for military and civil applications (scramjet combustors, hypersonic leading edges, turbine blades, rocket nozzles) but that most studies used gaseous coolant, leaving liquid-coolant phase-change effects underexplored, and that the prevailing TPMM relies on a local-thermal-equilibrium assumption of unverified validity for two-phase problems.
Related Works (click for detail)
Although the paper has no separate related-works section, the introduction surveys the relevant modeling lineage and prior phase-change transpiration-cooling studies. It contrasts the SPM with the TPMM, traces the LTE-based TPMM, and cites application-oriented and phenomenon-specific studies that this work builds on or contrasts against.
Method (click for detail)
The physical model is a one-dimensional porous matrix of thickness L whose upper surface receives heat flux Q while liquid coolant is injected from a reservoir at temperature T_c with mass flow rate m. The formulation couples the TPMM (enthalpy and saturation replacing fluid temperature, so one set of mass, momentum, and fluid-energy equations covers liquid, two-phase, and vapor regions) with an LTNE treatment that adds a separate solid-energy equation and an interfacial convective heat exchange term Q_sf. Constitutive relations (permeability, viscosity, advection correction, capillary/effective diffusion, relative mobilities, boiling source term) close the system, with four energy boundary conditions adapted from Wang and Shi (2008).
Experiment (click for detail)
Numerical experiments use pure water as coolant with saturated-water properties at 373.15 K and 1 atm (Table 2). The 1D domain (porous matrix thickness 10 cm) is discretized into uniform control volumes with staggered grids; grid independence is confirmed across 50, 100, and 200 cells, with 100 cells adopted. Parametric sweeps vary coolant mass flow rate, heat flux, solid thermal conductivity, porosity, sphere diameter, and hot-surface pressure to map temperature and saturation distributions.
Discussion (click for detail)
The results show that higher coolant mass flow rate delays evaporation, shrinks the two-phase and vapor regions, and lowers hot-surface solid temperature, while higher heat flux expands the two-phase and vapor regions. Higher solid thermal conductivity raises liquid-region temperature but lowers hot-surface temperature. Larger porosity or sphere diameter dilates the two-phase region (via increased capillary diffusion) and raises liquid temperature. The headline discovery is the inverse fluid-solid temperature phenomenon in the two-phase region — captured only by the LTNE-TPMM — and the finding that higher hot-surface pressure worsens cooling through reduced latent heat.
Conclusion (click for detail)
The authors conclude that the new LTNE-TPMM successfully models transpiration cooling with coolant phase change and reveals an inverse fluid-solid temperature phenomenon in the two-phase region that only this model can capture. They report that higher hot-surface pressure degrades cooling and that the two-phase behavior is controllable through porosity, sphere diameter, conductivity, and coolant injection rate.
